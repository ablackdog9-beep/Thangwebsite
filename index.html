What does a phage hold on to?

Thang Ngoc Tran — Lowary lab · mycobacteriophage–host interaction & bacterial genetic tools

A mycobacteriophage has to find one molecule on a cell wall that is mostly lipid, mostly impenetrable, and unlike almost any other bacterial surface. The question

Find the receptor by breaking it

Mycobacteria wrap themselves in a waxy, mycolic-acid outer layer that makes them hard to kill and hard to study. Phages infect them anyway, which means something on that surface is recognisable. Identifying it matters for phage therapy against M. tuberculosis and M. abscessus, and for understanding how the envelope is assembled in the first place.

My candidates are the glycopeptidolipids — the glycosylated lipids decorating the outermost layer of M. smegmatis, positioned exactly where a tail fibre would make first contact.

The approach is reductive: delete a step in the GPL biosynthesis pathway, confirm nothing else about the cell has quietly changed, then measure whether the phage still sticks. A mutant that grows normally but no longer adsorbs points at a receptor. Work comparing wild type with GPL-pathway deletion mutants across adsorption, infection and burst is in preparation.

Genetic toolkit

Three ways to silence a gene, and when each one earns its place

Mycobacteria recombine poorly and grow slowly, so the method matters as much as the target. I work across all three Cas9 modes plus classical allelic exchange, and choose by whether the gene is essential, whether a clean scar-free deletion is needed, and how fast an answer has to come.

cuts both strands

Cas9

Full knockouts and HDR knock-ins with a donor template. Fast, but a double-strand break in a slow grower is a blunt instrument.

nicks one strand

Cas9 nickase

Paired nicks on opposite strands give a staggered break with far lower off-target risk — worth the extra sgRNA when specificity decides the result.

binds, never cuts

dCas9 / CRISPRi

Knockdown instead of knockout. The only honest way to ask what an essential envelope gene does, and tunable enough to titrate the phenotype.

01Allelic exchange

In-frame, unmarked deletions by recombineering and counter-selection — still the cleanest genotype when the paper has to stand up.

02Transposon mutagenesis

Unbiased forward screening when the candidate list is empty rather than too long.

03Complementation

Every phenotype gets its gene put back. A mutant that is not complemented is a hypothesis, not a result.

How it is measured

Four read-outs, each answering a different objection

A single infection experiment proves very little on its own. Each assay below exists to close off one alternative explanation for why a mutant looks resistant.

Run together on the same strain pair, they separate the phage cannot bind from the phage binds but cannot replicate, and from the cell was already dying.

01One-step growth curve

Synchronised infection, then titre over a time course. Gives latent period and burst size — the difference between a phage that cannot get in and one that gets in but replicates badly.

02Killing assay

Viable host counts after challenge at a fixed MOI. Measures the outcome that actually matters: whether the phage clears the population.

03Cell viability — alamarBlue

Resazurin reduction as a metabolic read-out, in plate format. Catches the trivial explanation first: a mutant that looks resistant because it was already unhealthy.

04Confocal microscopy

Fluorescent reporter phage on fluorescently labelled host, single cell by single cell. Resolves what a bulk titre averages away — which cells were entered, and which were only touched.
